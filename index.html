    Vectors

    Mechanics runs on arrows and rates of change. Drag the two arrows to see how components, the dot product and the cross product respond.

    The intuition

    A vector is a set of directions: "go 3 east and 1.5 north." Adding vectors is following one set of directions, then the next.

    The dot product asks "how much do these point the same way?" Pushing a cart along its motion does a lot of work; pushing sideways does none. The cross product asks "how much do these twist?" A wrench turns best when you push perpendicular to it.

    2. Drag B so it points the same way as A.

    3. Move B from one side of A to the other.

    Worked example

    You pull a sled 20 m across level snow with a rope that makes 30° with the ground. The rope tension is 50 N. How much work do you do?

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Picture and FBD. The sled moves right. Forces: rope tension T at 30°, weight mg, normal force N, friction f.
    2. Write the vectors. T = (50 cos 30°, 50 sin 30°) = (43.3, 25) N, Δr = (20, 0) m.
    3. Dot product. W = T·Δr = 43.3·20 + 25·0 = 866 J.
    4. Same thing another way. W = Td cos θ = 50·20·cos 30° = 866 J. ✓
    5. Interpret. The vertical part of the pull does no work, because it is perpendicular to the motion. It only reduces N.

    Common mistakes

    • Adding lengths instead of components. |A + B| is usually less than |A| + |B|.
    • Leaving the calculator in degrees when a formula needs radians.
    • Forgetting that A · B is a number while A × B is a vector.

    The toolkit

    • Components. Ax = A cos θ, Ay = A sin θ. Add vectors by adding components. Almost every 2D problem becomes two 1D problems this way.
    • Dot product → work. W = F·Δr. Only the part of the force along the motion counts. Make A and B perpendicular and watch A·B hit zero.
    • Cross product → twisting. Torque τ = r × F and angular momentum L = r × p. Right-hand rule: fingers along the first vector, curl toward the second, thumb gives the direction.
    • Units as a lie detector. Check units on every answer. A pendulum's period can only depend on L and g as √(L/g), because that is the only combination with units of seconds.
    • Small angles. In radians, sin θ ≈ θ and cos θ ≈ 1 − θ²/2. This turns a pendulum into simple harmonic motion in Week 9.

    Derivatives: how fast things change

    A derivative tells you how fast something is changing right now. In physics it turns position into velocity and velocity into acceleration.

    The intuition

    Check your car's odometer twice, one second apart: the difference is your average speed over that second. Shrink the gap to a hundredth of a second, then a thousandth: the answer settles on one number. That limit is the derivative, your speed at that instant.

    On a graph it is the slope of the line that just touches the curve at one point, the tangent line.

    1. Shrink the gap h to its smallest value.

      What you should see

      The secant slope settles onto the tangent slope. That limiting value is the derivative.

    2. Pick sin t and move the point to a peak.

      What you should see

      At the top of the hill the tangent is flat: derivative 0. For motion, that is a turnaround point.

    3. Pick the exponential.

      What you should see

      Its slope is always exactly half its height: d(e^(t/2))/dt = ½e^(t/2).

    Worked example

    A mass on a spring has position x(t) = 5 sin(2t) cm. Find v(t), a(t), and the acceleration at t = 0.5 s.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    A pure math step: no forces needed here. (The spring's FBD is in Simple harmonic motion.)

    1. Identify the inside function. x = 5 sin(u) with u = 2t, so du/dt = 2.
    2. Velocity (chain rule). v = 5 cos(2t)·2 = 10 cos(2t) cm/s.
    3. Acceleration. a = −20 sin(2t) cm/s².
    4. At t = 0.5 s. a = −20 sin(1) = −16.8 cm/s² (radians!).
    5. Check. a = −4x: exactly SHM with ω² = 4. ✓

    Common mistakes

    • Forgetting the chain rule: d/dt sin(ωt) = ω cos(ωt), not cos(ωt).
    • Expecting a constant to survive. The derivative of x₀ is 0.
    • Confusing the value of a function with its slope.

    Integrals: adding up

    An integral adds up lots of tiny pieces. In physics it turns velocity into distance, force into work, and bits of mass into a moment of inertia.

    The intuition

    Drive at 30 mph for 2 hours and you go 60 miles: speed × time, the area of a rectangle. If your speed keeps changing, chop the trip into short pieces where the speed is nearly constant, add up the thin rectangles, and make them thinner and thinner.

    The total becomes the area under the speed graph. That is the integral.

    Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

    1. Use just 1 rectangle, then 60.

      What you should see

      One rectangle is a rough guess; 60 thin ones hug the curve and match the exact area.

    2. Pick 10 − 2t and integrate to t = 8.

      What you should see

      After t = 5 the velocity is negative: that area counts as negative (moving backward).

    3. Pick sin t and integrate a full cycle (to 6.28).

      What you should see

      The positive and negative areas cancel: zero net displacement.

    Worked example

    An object's velocity is v(t) = 3t² − 2t m/s. How far does it move between t = 1 s and t = 3 s?

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    A pure math step: no forces needed here.

    1. Set up. Displacement is the area under v: Δx = ∫13 (3t² − 2t) dt.
    2. Antiderivative. F(t) = t³ − t².
    3. Plug in the limits. F(3) − F(1) = (27 − 9) − (1 − 1) = 18 m.
    4. Distance or displacement? v(t) = t(3t − 2) is positive for all t > 2/3 s, so it never reverses on this interval: distance = 18 m too.
    5. Check. Average velocity 18 m / 2 s = 9 m/s, between v(1) = 1 and v(3) = 21 m/s. ✓

    Common mistakes

    • Forgetting the limits, or the starting value, when you integrate.
    • Reading area below the axis as positive. It means motion in the negative direction.
    • Using ∫v dt for distance when v changes sign. Distance is ∫|v| dt.

    Dot and cross products, step by step

    Two ways to multiply vectors. The dot product gives a number that measures how much two arrows point the same way. The cross product gives a new arrow that measures how much they twist.

    The intuition

    Dot product: shine a light straight down onto A and look at its shadow along B. Shadow length × length of B = A · B. Same direction: big and positive. Perpendicular: zero. Opposite: negative.

    Cross product: its size is the area of the parallelogram the two arrows make, and it points straight out of that parallelogram. Use your right hand: fingers along A, curl them toward B, and your thumb points along A × B.

    B

    Try this

    Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

    1. Make B point the same way as A.

      What you should see

      A × B becomes (0, 0, 0): parallel vectors don't twist anything.

    2. Make them perpendicular.

      What you should see

      A · B = 0, and |A × B| = |A||B|, its maximum.

    3. Swap A and B.

      What you should see

      The cross product flips direction; the dot product stays the same.

    Worked example

    A force F = (0, 3, 4) N acts at position r = (2, 1, 0) m from a pivot. Find the torque τ = r × F and its size.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Write the pattern. r × F = (ryFz − rzFy, rzFx − rxFz, rxFy − ryFx).
    2. x component. 1·4 − 0·3 = 4.
    3. y component. 0·0 − 2·4 = −8.
    4. z component. 2·3 − 1·0 = 6. So τ = (4, −8, 6) N·m.
    5. Size. |τ| = √(16 + 64 + 36) = 10.8 N·m.
    6. Check. τ must be perpendicular to r: r · τ = 2·4 + 1·(−8) + 0 = 0. ✓

    Common mistakes

    • Getting the order wrong. A × B = −(B × A).
    • Dropping the minus sign in the middle (y) term of the cross product.
    • Treating A · B as a vector. It is just a number.

    Differential equations you will meet

    Newton's second law is a differential equation: it connects a quantity to how fast it changes. Only a handful of types appear in this course, and they all have known answers.

    The intuition

    A differential equation is a rule for the slope at every point. Draw a little slope mark everywhere on a grid, drop a starting point, and follow the marks like a leaf floating down a stream. The path you trace is the solution.

    Different starting points give different paths, which is why every problem also needs an initial condition.

    Try this

    Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

    1. Terminal speed: start at different speeds.

      What you should see

      Every solution curve heads to the same terminal speed, from above or below.

    2. Decay: double k.

      What you should see

      The curve drops twice as fast. k sets the time scale, 1/k.

    3. Compare v² drag with linear drag.

      What you should see

      v² drag kills high speeds fast but has a long, slow tail at low speeds.

    Worked example

    A puck slows by quadratic drag: dv/dt = −kv², with v(0) = v₀. Find v(t).

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    A pure math step. The puck's FBD has one horizontal force, drag, pointing backward.

    1. Separate the variables. All v on one side, all t on the other: dv/v² = −k dt.
    2. Integrate both sides. ∫v₀v dv/v² = −k∫0t dt gives −1/v + 1/v0 = −kt.
    3. Solve for v. v = v0/(1 + kv0t).
    4. Check t = 0. v = v₀. ✓
    5. Check late times. v → 0 but slowly, like 1/t, unlike the fast exponential decay of linear drag.

    Common mistakes

    • Forgetting the initial condition. It picks which curve you are on.
    • Mixing variables when separating: get all the v's on one side and all the t's on the other.
    • Guessing the wrong form for a second-order equation. Use the table.

    The equations in this course

    Recipe for first-order ones: separate the variables, integrate both sides with limits, then solve for the unknown. For second-order ones, guess the form in the table and check it.

    Position, velocity and acceleration are one function and its derivatives. Slopes take you down the stack. Areas take you back up.

    A particle moves along x with position x(t) = t³ − 6t² + 9t (meters, seconds). (a) When is it at rest? (b) At t = 2.5 s, is it speeding up or slowing down?

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Picture and FBD. The particle moves on a line. Whatever the forces are, they add to one net force F = ma along x, pointing the same way as a.
    2. Velocity. v = dx/dt = 3t² − 12t + 9 = 3(t − 1)(t − 3).
    3. (a) At rest. v = 0 at t = 1 s and t = 3 s.
    4. Acceleration. a = dv/dt = 6t − 12. At t = 2.5 s: v = 3(1.5)(−0.5) = −2.25 m/s and a = +3 m/s².
    5. (b) Compare signs. v is negative and a is positive: opposite signs, so it is slowing down.
    6. Check. It stops at t = 3 s, so just before that it should indeed be slowing. ✓

    Common mistakes

    • Reading the height of a graph when the question asks about its slope or area.
    • Thinking negative acceleration always means slowing down. It only does when velocity is positive.
    • Treating distance and displacement as the same thing when the object turns around.

    Reading the graphs

    • Speeding up or slowing down? Compare the signs of v and a. Same sign: speeding up. Opposite signs: slowing down. A negative acceleration alone tells you nothing about speed.
    • At rest or turning around where v = 0. It turns around only if v changes sign there.
    • Displacement vs distance. Displacement is ∫v dt. Distance is ∫|v| dt, so split the integral at every zero of v. Try the Turnaround preset past t = 3 s and watch the two numbers separate.
    • Position from velocity needs a starting point. x(t) = x0 + ∫0t v(t′) dt′. Free-response questions often give x0 in a sentence you skim past.

    Integrate a constant a twice and you get every equation you need. Each one leaves out one of the five quantities, so pick the one that skips what you don't know and don't need.

    Worked example

    A car moving at 25 m/s brakes with its wheels locked and stops in 50 m. Find (a) the deceleration, (b) the stopping time, (c) the coefficient of kinetic friction.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    Where the equations come from

    Start from dv/dt = a with a constant. Integrating once gives v = v0 + at. Integrating again gives x = x0 + v0t + ½at². Eliminate t between them and you get v² = v0² + 2aΔx.

    Free fall is the special case a = −g with up as positive, g = 9.8 m/s². The acceleration is −9.8 m/s² on the way up, at the top and on the way down. At the top, v = 0 but a ≠ 0.

    Which way does friction point?

    The most common free-body-diagram mistake is drawing friction the wrong way. One test settles it every time: on ramps, under wheels, on rolling balls and under ladders.

    The intuition: the ice test

    Static. The top block speeds up to the right, and friction from the bottom block is the only horizontal force on it, so it points right. By the 3rd law, the top block pushes the bottom one to the left.

    7. A ball rolls DOWN a ramp without slipping.

    Static (the contact point is momentarily at rest). Ice test: on an icy ramp the ball would slide down without spinning, so its contact point would slip down. Friction points up the slope, and that is what makes it spin faster as it speeds up.

    8. A ball rolls UP a ramp without slipping, slowing down.

    Static. This surprises most people: still UP the slope. Gravity slows the ball's forward motion but can't slow its spin. Without friction the ball would keep spinning too fast for its speed, and its contact point would slip down the slope. Friction points up to slow the spin.

    9. A car speeds up from rest on a flat road. The tires grip.

    Static (tires grip). The engine spins the driven wheels. On ice they would spin in place, their bottoms sliding backward. So friction points forward: the road pushes the car forward. That is the force that accelerates every car.

    10. A car brakes so hard the wheels lock and it skids.

    Kinetic (the tires slide). The locked tires slide forward over the road, so kinetic friction points backward.

    11. A bowling ball is released sliding forward with no spin.

    Kinetic (its bottom slides along the lane). Its bottom slides forward, so kinetic friction points backward. That friction slows the ball and also spins it up, until v = ωR. Then it rolls and the friction stops.

    12. A ball rolls at constant speed across a level floor.

    None (ideal rolling). Nothing needs to change: no speeding up, no slowing down, and the spin already matches the speed. So no friction is needed. (Real balls slow slightly because of rolling resistance.)

    13. A ladder leans against a frictionless wall. Which way does the floor's friction push on the ladder's foot?

    Static. Ice test: on an icy floor the foot would slide away from the wall and the ladder would fall. Friction points toward the wall. A torque balance about the foot tells you how big it must be.

    14. A coin rides on a turntable spinning at a steady rate. The coin doesn't slip.

    Static. The coin moves in a circle, so it needs an inward force. Friction is the only horizontal force, so it points toward the center. On ice the coin would fly off in a straight line.

    Common mistakes

    • "Friction always points opposite the motion." True for kinetic friction. Static friction opposes the tendency to slip, which can be along the motion (a car speeding up, a ball rolling up a ramp).
    • Using μₛN for static friction automatically. It is only that big on the verge of slipping.
    • Giving rolling objects kinetic friction. If there is no slipping, the contact point is at rest, so it is static.
    • Forgetting the 3rd-law partner: if the floor pushes the ladder toward the wall, the ladder pushes the floor away from it.

    The rules in one place

    • Kinetic: fk = μkN, opposite the sliding of the contact point relative to the surface.
    • Static: fs ≤ μsN, direction from the ice test, size from ΣF = ma (and Στ = Iα when things spin).
    • Ramps: holding a box still with no push, friction points up the slope. Push hard enough and it flips to point down.
    • Rolling: rolling down or up a ramp, static friction points up the slope. On level ground at constant speed, none. Driven wheels speeding up: forward. Braking without skidding: backward.
    • Slipping: compare v with ωR. If v > ωR the bottom slides forward (friction back). If ωR > v it slides backward (friction forward).
    • Extended objects (ladders, rods): find the direction with the ice test at each contact, then get the size from a torque balance.

    • When to use energy: the question asks about speed or height, not time. Use forces or kinematics when you need time or direction.

    Momentum and collisions

    In a collision the forces are huge, brief and messy. Momentum lets you skip them entirely: whatever one object gains, the other loses.

    Newton's law of gravity plus F = ma produces all three of Kepler's laws. Launch a satellite sideways and watch them appear.

    A wave moves a pattern, not the stuff. Each bit of string only goes up and down, yet the shape races along.

    The intuition

    Think of a stadium wave: every fan just stands and sits, but the wave circles the stadium. On a string, each piece copies the motion of its neighbor a moment later.

    How fast the pattern moves depends only on the string: tighter strings snap back faster, heavier strings respond slower, so v = √(T/μ). Shake faster and you don't speed the wave up; you just pack the wiggles closer together.

    4. Wavelength. λ = v/f = 63/50 = 1.26 m.
    5. Check. Shaking faster would shorten λ but not change v. ✓

    Common mistakes

    • Confusing wave speed with how fast a bit of string moves up and down.
    • Thinking a higher frequency makes the wave travel faster.
    • Mixing up k and λ. k = 2π/λ.

    More about waves

    • Any shape y = f(x − vt) moves right at speed v without changing; f(x + vt) moves left. Both satisfy the wave equation.
    • Transverse (string, light): wiggle perpendicular to travel. Longitudinal (sound): wiggle along it.
    • Superposition: overlapping waves add. Waves pass through each other unchanged.
    • Reflection: from a fixed end the pulse comes back upside down; from a free end it comes back upright.
    • Energy: a sine wave on a string carries power P = ½μω²A²v.

    Interference

    Two waves in the same place add up. Depending on their timing, they can make each other louder or cancel out.

    The intuition

    Two speakers play the same note. Where crests from both arrive together, the sound is loud; where a crest meets a trough, they cancel. Which happens depends only on the difference in distance to the two speakers: a whole number of wavelengths is loud, a half-number is quiet.

    Loud where d sin θ = mλ (sources in phase). Shift one source by 180° and the center line goes quiet.

    Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

    1. Shift one source by half a cycle.

      What you should see

      The center line, which was loud, goes quiet.

    2. Spread the sources apart.

      What you should see

      More loud directions, packed closer together.

    3. Show the average loudness.

      What you should see

      The moving ripples disappear, leaving bright loud stripes and pale quiet ones.

    Worked example

    Two speakers 3.0 m apart play the same 343 Hz tone in phase. Sound travels at 343 m/s. Far away, at what angles is it first quiet, and first loud again, off the center line?

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    No forces in this problem: interference is about how two waves add, not about pushes on an object.

    1. Picture. Two sources, a far-away listener at angle θ. The path difference is d sin θ.
    2. Wavelength. λ = 343/343 = 1.0 m.
    3. First quiet spot. Half a wavelength of path difference: 3.0 sin θ = 0.5, θ = 9.6°.
    4. First loud spot off center. 3.0 sin θ = 1.0, θ = 19.5°.
    5. Check. Quiet comes before loud as you move off center, as it should. ✓

    Common mistakes

    • Using path length instead of path difference.
    • Forgetting the half-wavelength for quiet spots.
    • Assuming the center is always loud. A phase shift changes that.

    Path difference is everything

    • Loud (constructive): path difference Δr = mλ, crest meets crest.
    • Quiet (destructive): Δr = (m + ½)λ, crest meets trough.
    • Far away the path difference is d sin θ, so the loud directions are at d sin θ = mλ.
    • Phase shift: if one source starts half a cycle late, loud and quiet swap. Noise-cancelling headphones use exactly this.

    Check yourself: two speakers play in phase. A spot is 1.5 wavelengths farther from one speaker than the other. What do you hear there?

    Standing waves

    Trap a wave between two ends and only certain wavelengths fit. That is why a guitar string or a flute plays definite notes instead of noise.

    The intuition

    A guitar string is pinned at both ends, so whatever pattern it makes must have zero motion there. Only whole numbers of half-wavelengths fit: one hump, two humps, three. Each fit is a note, and together they're the harmonic series.

    A pipe works the same way with sound: the air inside sloshes back and forth, and the ends decide which patterns fit. A closed end blocks the air; an open end lets it move freely.

    2. Wavelength. λ1 = 2L = 1.30 m.
    3. Wave speed. v = fλ = 110·1.30 = 143 m/s.
    4. Second harmonic. Two humps, f2 = 2f1 = 220 Hz, one octave higher.
    5. Check. Harmonics of a string are whole-number multiples of the fundamental. ✓

    Common mistakes

    • Putting nodes at the open end of a pipe. Open ends are antinodes.
    • Using v/(2L) for a pipe closed at one end. It is v/(4L).
    • Thinking a longer string plays higher.

    Check yourself: a pipe closed at one end can play which harmonics?

    Beats and the Doppler effect

    Two everyday effects of waves in time: the pulsing of two almost-matching notes, and the pitch shift of a moving siren.

    The intuition

    Two notes slightly out of tune drift in and out of step. When they line up the sound is loud, when they are opposite it is quiet, so the loudness pulses: beats.

    A siren driving toward you squashes its waves together in front, so you hear a higher pitch. As it drives away, the waves behind it get stretched out and the pitch drops.

    Try this

    Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

    1. Tune the two tones to match.

      What you should see

      The beats vanish.

    2. Pull them 2 Hz apart.

      What you should see

      Two loudness pulses per second.

    3. Push the source past the speed of sound.

      What you should see

      The wavefronts pile into a cone, a shock wave.

    Worked example

    An ambulance with a 500 Hz siren drives toward you at 30 m/s, passes, and drives away. Sound travels at 343 m/s. What frequencies do you hear?

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Picture and FBD. Constant velocity, so the forces balance. The pitch change comes from the motion of the source through the air, not from any force.
    2. Approaching. Waves get bunched up: f′ = 500·343/(343 − 30) = 548 Hz.
    3. Receding. Waves get stretched: f′ = 500·343/(343 + 30) = 460 Hz.
    4. The drop you hear. About 88 Hz as it passes.
    5. Check. Toward you is higher, away is lower. ✓

    Common mistakes

    • Adding the two frequencies to get the beat frequency.
    • Doppler sign mix-ups. Moving toward each other always raises the pitch.
    • Thinking the source changes the frequency it emits. It is the spacing of the waves that changes.

    The formulas

    • Beats: fbeat = |f1 − f2|, at an average pitch (f1 + f2)/2.
    • Doppler: f′ = f(v ± vo)/(v ∓ vs). Choose the signs so moving toward each other raises the pitch.
    • Moving source vs moving listener: the formulas differ slightly because sound travels through air, so motion relative to the air matters.
    • Shock waves: when the source outruns its own sound, the wavefronts pile up into a cone, heard as a sonic boom.

    Fourier series

    Any repeating signal can be built out of pure sine waves. That idea is behind how your ear hears pitch and how music files store sound.

    The intuition

    Any repeating shape is a recipe of pure sine tones: the fundamental plus harmonics at 2, 3, 4… times the frequency. Smooth shapes need only a pinch of the high harmonics. Sharp corners and jumps need lots of them. That recipe is why a violin and a flute playing the same note sound different.

    Fourier series: building shapes from pure tones
    Highest harmonic—
    Peak overshoot—

    Top: the target (dashed) and the sum so far (blue), with the first few individual sines faint. Bottom: the spectrum, how much of each harmonic goes in. Near the jump the sum always overshoots by about 9% of the jump, which is about 18% of the amplitude, no matter how many terms you add. More terms squeeze the overshoot closer to the jump but never remove it. This is called the Gibbs phenomenon.

    Try this

    Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

    1. Build a square wave from 40 terms.

      What you should see

      Sharp corners, but the overshoot at the jump never goes away.

    2. Switch to a triangle with only 3 terms.

      What you should see

      Already almost perfect. Smooth shapes need few harmonics.

    3. Try the sawtooth.

      What you should see

      It needs every harmonic, even and odd.

    Worked example

    A square wave swings between +1 and −1 at 100 Hz. List the first three nonzero harmonics and their amplitudes.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    No forces in this problem: Fourier series is about how a signal is built from sine waves.

    1. Picture. A square wave is symmetric, so only odd harmonics appear: (4/π)(sin ωt + ⅓ sin 3ωt + ⅕ sin 5ωt + …).
    2. First harmonic. 100 Hz, amplitude 4/π = 1.27.
    3. Third harmonic. 300 Hz, amplitude 4/(3π) = 0.42.
    4. Fifth harmonic. 500 Hz, amplitude 4/(5π) = 0.25.
    5. Check. The fundamental is larger than the square wave itself (1.27 > 1). The higher harmonics trim its peaks down. ✓

    Common mistakes

    • Thinking only odd harmonics ever appear. That is special to symmetric shapes like the square wave.
    • Expecting the overshoot to vanish with enough terms.
    • Confusing harmonic number with amplitude.

    The recipe

    • A repeating signal with period T is a sum f(t) = Σ bn sin(nωt) + an cos(nωt), with ω = 2π/T.
    • The list of amplitudes is the spectrum: how much of each harmonic goes in.
    • Timbre: a flute and a violin playing the same note share the fundamental but have different spectra.
    • Sharp corners and jumps need lots of high harmonics. Smooth shapes are mostly the fundamental.

    Check yourself: which shape needs the most high harmonics to build accurately?
